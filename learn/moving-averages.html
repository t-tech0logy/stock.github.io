Price direction

Moving averages without the jargon.

A moving average turns a noisy sequence of daily prices into a smoother line. That makes direction easier to see, but it cannot predict what comes next.

The simple idea

A moving average smooths out daily price noise. It answers a basic question: has the typical price been moving up or down?

A 50-day average uses the latest 50 closing prices. A 200-day average uses 200, so it changes more slowly.

What this shows: the 50-day line follows recent price changes sooner. The 200-day line is steadier because each new day has less influence on a much larger group of prices.

How to read it

A price above a rising average usually means the recent direction has been upward. A price below a falling average usually means the direction has been downward.

Three common chart situations
↗Above + risingRecent direction is supportive
→Near + flatThe direction is unclear
↘Below + fallingRecent direction is weak

How to use it: treat these as descriptions of the past—not automatic buy or sell instructions.

It looks backward.

The line changes only after the price changes. It cannot predict tomorrow and it is not the company's true value.

How PlainStock uses them

PlainStock checks both averages as one part of the score. No moving average can decide whether a stock is good, cheap, or right for you.

  • Is the average rising, falling, or flat?
  • Is the latest price close to it or far away?
  • Does the real business support the trend?
